## Let's look at a model of the decision-making process

{{< decision-process >}}

## Overview

What we're seeing here is what happens over a period of time. First, an event happens that triggers the decision-making process. Then we see our dog's natural reflex to the trigger, followed by an emotional reaction to the trigger, and finally the physical response they decide on to deal with the trigger. When this response is reinforced, it becomes a habit. This habit is what we call a behavior. When we create behaviors proactively, we start with the event and move our way to the response. When we are modifying a behavior, we start by breaking the habit and work our way back to the event.

_To best explain this, let's use an example._

## Example

We are walking our dog, and as our paths cross another dog, our dog sees this dog. We notice a change in their walk, their hackles go up, and shortly after that we see barking and lunging. As a result, we decide our dog is afraid of other dogs, so we try to comfort him/her and keep the other dog away. We mean well, but because we are thinking about the wrong step of the decision-making process, we are actually reinforcing the response. We repeat this, and the dog's reactions get worse; the barking turns to aggression. The dog develops a habit of barking at anything that makes it uncomfortable. We just keep trying to comfort him/her, and the habit is formed.

### Habit and its relationship with reinforcement {#habit}

In the example above, we were trying to fix the dog's emotional reaction, to calm their fear. But because our dogs don't know English, all they learned is that this response gets them what they want. Our first step is breaking this habit, which means making sure we avoid reinforcing their negative response. When they decide to bark at the approaching dog to make it go away, or to get pets from us, we do not remove the trigger from the board, and we do not give treats or pets.

Instead, we want to give the dog an alternative behavior to get past the trigger. In this case, since we are walking our dog outside, it could be putting the dog in a focused heel, or putting the dog in a down and letting the other dog walk by. When our dog starts performing the new, acceptable behavior, we can reinforce it with pets, treats, or play and create a new habit.

### Response

The response is the physical decision the dog makes about the trigger. In our example, we're talking about barking at an approaching dog. Before we work on our dog's emotional reaction, which is fear, we need to deal with the response the dog is giving. Since the dog is performing an inappropriate behavior we want to stop, the correct response from us is to give the dog a correction. The correction should be an uncomfortable stress that is strong enough to redirect the dog away from their barking. As soon as we stop the barking, we want to use pressure to bring the dog into an acceptable behavior. The easiest one here is to use leash pressure to put our dog in a down and ask the other dog's handler to keep their distance as they walk by.

The tricky part is that we want to apply our correction at the earliest point in the response. Before our dog lunges at a dog, they bark. Before they bark, they get that evil stare and straight tail. Before that, their hackles go up. The hackles are part of the dog's reflex, and we don't care about that here. We apply our correction at the death stare.

The earlier in the response we apply our correction, the less pressure we need to stop the behavior. I'll help you find the best time to apply corrections.

This step might feel stressful to some handlers, but it's actually where you'll see improvement fastest. This step is where we focus our initial efforts, and you'll see that a new possibility exists when you're out walking your dog.

### Reaction

In this example, our dog's reaction to seeing other dogs is fear. Fear is a common cause of reactivity, and it's harder to fix than predatory aggression. For fear, we need to expose the dog to the event causing the anxiety at lower intensities until the dog starts to habituate (_desensitize_) to the situation. We do this by bringing them within sight of other dogs, but at a distance below our dog's threshold, and showing them that nothing bad happens to them. I like to do this with play/food training at a distance, slowly bringing the dog closer to the other dogs.

Play/food training works great for healing fear, but it needs to happen before the response. And not just before the barking: we want to stop it before the initial response. If the death stare has started, we need to go back to dealing with our dog's response. If the dog is looking to us for treats or play and we are the object of their attention, then we encourage that. We reward and reinforce it.

This is the part that takes the longest to fix, but you won't need me for long. You can always hire me to help you find your dog's threshold and expand it, but my goal is to help you get comfortable finding it on your own and to develop a schedule you can keep up with to help your dog overcome this fear.

### Reflex

Reflexes are outside our control. In our example, this is the dog's hackles. Reflexes change with the dog's reaction. The important note here is that a reflex is outside our dog's control. You should **NEVER** apply a correction to a reflex alone. It is not a response, and it doesn't mean the dog is going to get aggressive. It means the dog is getting some cortisol in its system and the nervous system is preparing for fight or flight. Don't worry about this step; it will fix itself with the reaction training.

### Event

We can use management and walk our dogs when or where we won't see other dogs. This is acceptable, but the world is full of events. The environment will never stop trying to stimulate your dog in some way. Managing the events your dog is exposed to is a viable strategy for some people. Since you are here, I assume you aren't one of them. Neither am I. Together, we'll teach your dog to handle whatever the world throws at them appropriately. The result will be a well-mannered dog with the freedom to do more.

Let's expose our friends to the world and teach them how to move through it.

## A note on prerequisites

You may have noticed that we need a few skills before we get started. We can't just go out and start breaking the habit on the first day. First, we need to establish some skills and tools to overcome different situations.

1. First, your dog needs a working relationship with you, me, and anyone else involved. We might need to muzzle or crate train the dog before it's safe for me to work with you and your pet. I'm pretty good at reading dogs, so I can get us through this step, but it might take more than one session just to get started. If your dog is cool with you, they'll be cool with me too. If we need to start with relationship building, I'll show you how I fast-forward through all the steps above so that we can get to a workable state.
2. Our dog needs behaviors trained with food/play. We want a trusting relationship. We want a place to bring the dog's attention, so we bring the engagement to us. This is required for getting through our dog's emotional reaction.
3. Our dog needs behaviors trained with leash pressure. Without a proper understanding of [escape and avoidance training](/faq/escape-and-avoidance/), our dog's response will overshadow our engagement every time we cross their threshold. A "_[force-free](/faq/training-styles/)_" trainer will focus on the reaction phase of this process. That's fine, but know that it will take orders of magnitude longer, and the engagement our dogs need to keep their attention will likely be more than you want in most situations. And not all dogs are trainable this way.
