Escape and avoidance training covers the other two corners of [operant conditioning](https://www.khanacademy.org/test-prep/mcat/behavior/learning-slug/v/operant-conditioning-positive-and-negative-reinforcement-and-punishment): [negative reinforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement#Negative_reinforcement) and [positive punishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punishment_(psychology)#Positive). Sometimes called Traditional dog training, this is where we tell a dog to do something and enforce the rules with some sort of pressure. The pressure I use most is leash pressure with a [pinch collar](/faq/pricing/#equipment).

{{< quadrant focus="trad" >}}

The dog first learns to *escape* light pressure by doing the behavior, then to [*avoid*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avoidance_learning) it entirely by doing the behavior before the pressure ever arrives. See [What is escape and avoidance training?](/faq/escape-and-avoidance/) for more. You'll need some [equipment](/faq/pricing/#equipment) before we start.

It's important to point out that new escape and avoidance training is highly stressful for the dog. We see the signs of a [cortisol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortisol) spike: ears go back, the tail goes stiff or tucks under, and resistance to the training is high. As the novelty wears off, though, the fear and anxiety go away. Done correctly, it actually becomes rewarding to the dog. [Here's why.](#why-it-becomes-rewarding)

## How I use pressure

Training with aversives is a hard sell, mostly because people misunderstand how they're used and how intense they need to be. I don't use hitting, [alpha rolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_roll), yelling, or social pressure. Those punishers are unclear, too intense, and give the dog no way to escape or avoid them. I consider all of them inhumane, yelling included. But the world is full of aversives, and using them properly is usually the fastest way to solve the problem you came to me with. It can also save your dog's life.

Two rules keep it safe:

- **The pressure must always be escapable.** Pressure the dog can't escape leads to fear and [learned helplessness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learned_helplessness).
- **Start low.** We begin below the level that changes behavior and raise it only until we see results. It's easier than it sounds, and I'll demonstrate with my own dogs.

Leash pressure isn't the only form of escape and avoidance training. It just has the most uses. We'll likely use a correction collar of some kind and teach your dog to follow the pressure. Anything your dog isn't comfortable with can be worked through the same way: force fetch, being picked up, grooming, and [muzzle training](/faq/pricing/#equipment) can all be aversive to your dog.

## Correction markers

You'll notice the pressure I use on my dogs is extremely light. That's because my correction markers are already conditioned. Like [reward markers](/services/food-and-play-training/#reward-markers), correction markers get stronger with use, so a calm *no* can work far better than a screamed "F\*$# STOP, FIDO!" See [What are verbal markers?](/faq/verbal-markers/)

## Commands you can count on

The commands your dog learned with food and play (*sit*, *down*, *place*, *here*) become reliable here. *Down* is a command, but it's also a continuation marker: your dog stays in the down to avoid pressure, even when something more exciting than you shows up. We proof commands through stress and in highly stimulating environments, so they hold up where food and play alone can't compete.

## What we train

### Recall

[Food and play](/services/food-and-play-training/#recall) makes recall fast and fun. This is where we bulletproof it. We need an environment stimulating enough to call for some negative reinforcement or a correction. Neither of us wants your dog to ignore a recall in a dangerous situation. The aversives I use are controlled and won't harm your dog. The aversives the world uses to enforce a recall can be fatal, so we do this right.

### Leash pulling and heeling

Leash pressure is how we fix leash pulling. It also turns a [heel](/services/food-and-play-training/#heeling) into something your dog holds around other dogs, traffic, and distractions, not just when the treats come out.

### Settling

The goal is to "flatten" your dog: lower their excitement, and teach them to settle even when aroused. We add distance and duration to *place*, *down*, and *sit*, and fade the leash so the behavior holds without it.

## Why it becomes rewarding

Successfully avoiding something unpleasant runs through the brain's reward system, the same [dopamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dopamine) system that responds to food and play. In rats trained to avoid a signaled footshock, the warning cue produced a burst of dopamine in the [nucleus accumbens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleus_accumbens) that predicted the rat would successfully avoid it. When a rat failed and only escaped, dopamine dipped instead ([Oleson et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3087-12.2012)). A follow-up study found that the pattern of dopamine during unpleasant events predicted which rats would learn to avoid them, with dopamine acting like a "safety" signal: things went better than expected ([Stelly et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1904249116)).

A 2025 study in mice found that different parts of the nucleus accumbens handle different stages of avoidance learning. Early on, dopamine responds to the unpleasant event itself. As learning progresses, it shifts to the warning cue, the moment the animal can act to stay safe ([Lopez et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.04.006)).

These are lab studies in rodents, not dogs, but the takeaway carries over: once your dog knows how to turn the pressure off, getting it right feels good. That's why the stress fades with practice and why a well-conditioned dog works confidently, not fearfully.

## What to expect

**Strengths**

- Bulletproofs behaviors
- Breaks bad habits
- Works faster than food and play for simple movements and behaviors
- Lowers your dog's energy
- Most importantly, teaches your dog how to handle stress

**Without food and play training**

- Can make a dog look and act suppressed
- Done incorrectly, can traumatize your dog

Without escape and avoidance training, we can't teach a dog how to handle stress. When a new stimulus shows up, the dog loses their mind and acts out on instinct, which is usually inappropriate.
